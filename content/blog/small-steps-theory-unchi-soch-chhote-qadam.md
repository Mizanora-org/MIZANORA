On Muaaz Iqbal's profile there is a line that sums up his whole method: **unchi soch, chhote qadam** — think high, take small steps. It sounds like a poster. It is actually a schedule.

![Muaaz Iqbal, founder and developer of Mizanora](/img/muaaz/muaaz-iqbal-02.jpg "Unchi soch, chhote qadam: the small steps theory")

## The theory

> Pick a goal too big for your current resources. Then make today's step so small that it is impossible to skip.

Most advice picks one half. Motivational culture gives you the big goal and no method. Productivity culture gives you the method and no goal. The small steps theory insists you need both, held together on purpose.

## What the two halves actually mean

**Unchi soch (think high).** When Muaaz started, "high" meant a Pakistani online store that a shopper in a small city could trust. He was a student. He had no warehouse, no team, no budget. The goal was not realistic — and that is the point. A realistic goal would have been a store for Kasur only, and it would have produced a smaller shop.

**Chhote qadam (small steps).** The steps were never "build the marketplace". They were: get one product page right. Then make photos load on a slow phone. Then add cash on delivery. Then add Urdu voice. Each one is a single sitting. The full sequence is written up in [how Mizanora was built](/blog/muaaz-iqbal-story-how-mizanora-was-built).

## The daily shape

The method has a shape you can copy exactly:

1. **One unchi goal, written down, unchanged for months.** Mizanora's goal has not moved since the beginning.
2. **One chhota qadam per session, small enough to finish.** If a step cannot be finished in one sitting, it is two steps.
3. **Ship the small step the same day.** Mizanora has shipped something almost every day for a long time — you can see the rhythm in the [live GitHub feed](/muaaz-iqbal).
4. **Never break the chain twice.** A missed day is a fact. Two missed days are a habit.

## Why this beats a big plan

A big plan has a hidden cost: it is only correct until reality disagrees with it. The first customer who cannot find the checkout invalidates six weeks of planning. Small steps get feedback after step one, so the plan is rewritten continuously by reality instead of by a document.

There is also a psychological trick. A big goal produces anxiety, and anxiety produces planning instead of work. A tiny, finishable step produces a completed thing, and completed things produce momentum. Muaaz does not rely on motivation; he relies on the fact that the next step is small enough that motivation is not required.

## What "small" really means

Small does not mean trivial. It means **independently finishable**. "Add Urdu voice to the assistant" is not a step. "Get the browser to list the phone's Urdu voices" is a step. The first takes a month and cannot be finished in a bad week; the second takes an afternoon and can.

This is also why the [zero-quota theory](/blog/zero-quota-theory-systems-that-survive-free-limits) matters: a system built from small, independent pieces survives the loss of any one piece. Big monolithic plans do not.

## The part nobody mentions

Small steps need a big goal to be worth anything, because steps only compound in a direction. An hour a day pointed nowhere is an hour a day. An hour a day pointed at the same target for a year is [the compound-builder theory](/blog/compound-builder-theory-one-hour-every-day), and it is how a student in Kasur ends up with a marketplace, a programming language and a live TV app.

> Direction is free. Consistency is expensive. Everybody buys the wrong one.
